# Class 1 — Bayesian Networks

Bayesian networks represent a joint probability distribution through a **directed acyclic graph (DAG)** and local conditional probability distributions.

## 1. Core idea

A Bayesian network factorizes the joint distribution as:

$$
P(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(X_i\mid Pa(X_i))
$$

<NoteBlock type="connection" title="Useful connection — graph and probability distribution">
The graph is not only a visualization. It specifies which local conditional distributions are needed and which conditional-independence assumptions are being made.
</NoteBlock>

## 2. Medical diagnosis example

<NetworkDiagram variant="medical" />

For influenza $I$, pneumonia $P$, fever $F$, cough $C$, and test $T$:

$$
P(I,P,F,C,T)=P(I)P(P)P(F\mid I,P)P(C\mid I,P)P(T\mid P)
$$

Once pneumonia is known, the network says the other non-parent variables give no extra information about the test:

$$
T\perp\!\!\!\perp \{I,F,C\}\mid P
$$

## 3. Conditional independence

$X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $Z$ when:

$$
P(X,Y\mid Z)=P(X\mid Z)P(Y\mid Z)
$$

Equivalently:

$$
P(X\mid Y,Z)=P(X\mid Z)
$$

<NoteBlock type="definition" title="Simplified definition — conditional independence">
Once $Z$ is known, learning $Y$ gives no additional information about $X$.
</NoteBlock>

## 4. Joint probability of three variables

The full joint probability is:

$$
P(X,Y,Z)
$$

Using the chain rule:

$$
P(X,Y,Z)=P(X\mid Y,Z)P(Y\mid Z)P(Z)
$$

For the chain $X\to Z\to Y$:

$$
P(X,Y,Z)=P(X)P(Z\mid X)P(Y\mid Z)
$$

## 5. Three path patterns

### Chain

$$
X\to Z\to Y
$$

Observing $Z$ blocks the path.

### Fork

$$
X\leftarrow Z\to Y
$$

Observing $Z$ blocks the path.

### Collider

$$
X\to Z\leftarrow Y
$$

A collider is blocked by default. Observing the collider, or one of its descendants, can **open** the path.

<NetworkDiagram variant="chain" />

### Example: Cloudy → Rainy → Wet

Without knowing whether it rained:

$$
P(Wet\mid Cloudy)\neq P(Wet)
$$

Once rain is observed:

$$
Cloudy\perp\!\!\!\perp Wet\mid Rainy
$$

## 6. Explaining away

<NetworkDiagram variant="collider" />

Burglary and earthquake can both cause an alarm:

$$
B\to A\leftarrow E
$$

with phone call as a descendant:

$$
A\to C
$$

The joint distribution is:

$$
P(B,E,A,C)=P(B)P(E)P(A\mid B,E)P(C\mid A)
$$

Before observing the alarm, burglary and earthquake may be independent. After observing the alarm, they become competing explanations:

$$
P(B\mid A,E)\neq P(B\mid A)
$$

Observing the phone call can have the same qualitative effect because it is a descendant of the collider.

<NoteBlock type="definition" title="Simplified definition — explaining away">
If two causes can explain the same observed effect, evidence for one cause can reduce the need for the other cause as an explanation.
</NoteBlock>

## 7. D-separation

D-separation answers whether information can still flow between two variables after conditioning on evidence.

- Chain/fork: observed middle node blocks.
- Collider: unobserved middle node blocks.
- Observed collider, or observed descendant of collider: can open.
- If **all paths** are blocked, the variables are d-separated and conditionally independent given the evidence.

<NoteBlock type="explanation" title="Further explanation — practical checklist">
Check every path between the variables. A single active path is enough for d-separation to fail.
</NoteBlock>

## 8. Evidence and reasoning direction

Arrows specify how the joint distribution factorizes; they do **not** limit inference to the arrow direction.

For example:

$$
P(Disease\mid Symptom)
=
\frac{P(Symptom\mid Disease)P(Disease)}{P(Symptom)}
$$

So the same network supports predictive, diagnostic, and intercausal reasoning.

## 9. Local and global independence

The local Markov property is:

$$
X_i\perp\!\!\!\perp NonDesc(X_i)\mid Pa(X_i)
$$

The global view uses d-separation to derive conditional independencies between arbitrary groups of variables.

<NoteBlock type="connection" title="Useful connection">
Think of the same model in three views: **graph → factorization → conditional independencies**.
</NoteBlock>

## 10. Inference tasks

Typical queries are:

$$
P(X),\qquad P(X\mid E=e),\qquad \arg\max_x P(x\mid E=e)
$$

These correspond to marginal probability, posterior probability, and most probable explanation.
