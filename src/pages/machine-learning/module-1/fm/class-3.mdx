# Class 3 — Statistical Estimation Theory

These notes cover the currently recorded parts of Chapter 2: Parzen-window density estimation, Gaussian mixtures, and expectation–maximization.

<SlideDeck src="slides/machine-learning/module-1/fm/chapter-2.pdf" title="Chapter 2 slides" />

## Essential summary

- Parzen windows estimate probability density locally from nearby samples.
- Kernel width controls how local or smooth the estimate is.
- A Gaussian mixture represents a density as a weighted sum of Gaussian components.
- Component assignments are latent variables.
- EM alternates between estimating responsibilities and updating model parameters.
- The same EM principle can handle missing variables when the unobserved quantities are treated probabilistically.

## 1. Parzen window density estimation

<div class="section-source"><SlideReference src="slides/machine-learning/module-1/fm/chapter-2.pdf" page={51} to={54} /></div>

### Region and indicator window

For a query point $x^*$, define a local region with side length $h$. In $n$ dimensions, its volume is:

$$
V=h^n
$$

An indicator window $gamma$ returns 1 for samples inside the region and 0 otherwise.

### Counting nearby samples

If $K$ of $N$ observations fall inside the region:

$$
K=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\gamma\!\left(\frac{x_k-x^*}{h}\right)
$$

and the density estimate is:

$$
\hat p(x^*)=\frac{K}{NV}
=\frac{1}{Nh^n}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\gamma\!\left(\frac{x_k-x^*}{h}\right)
$$

<NoteBlock type="explanation">
Dividing by $N$ converts the local count into a proportion. Dividing by the window volume converts that proportion into density per unit of space.
</NoteBlock>

### Smooth kernels

<div class="section-source"><SlideReference src="slides/machine-learning/module-1/fm/chapter-2.pdf" page={53} /></div>

A smooth kernel replaces the hard in/out count with graded contributions:

$$
\hat p(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{V(h)}
\gamma\!\left(\frac{x-x_k}{h}\right)
$$

Nearby observations can contribute more than distant ones.

### Conditions for a valid density

<div class="section-source"><SlideReference src="slides/machine-learning/module-1/fm/chapter-2.pdf" page={54} /></div>

A normalized nonnegative kernel satisfies:

$$
\gamma(u)\ge 0,
\qquad
\int_{\mathbb R^n}\gamma(u)\,du=1
$$

<NoteBlock type="definition">
A probability density may exceed 1 at a point. What must equal 1 is the integral of the density over the full domain.
</NoteBlock>

## 2. Gaussian mixture models

<div class="section-source"><SlideReference src="slides/machine-learning/module-1/fm/chapter-2.pdf" page={68} to={69} /></div>

A Gaussian mixture with $M$ components is:

$$
p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}P_i\,\mathcal N(x\mid m_i,\Sigma_i)
$$

Each component has:
- mixture weight $P_i$
- mean $m_i$
- covariance $\Sigma_i$

The component that generated each observation is not observed directly. That hidden assignment is a **latent variable**.

<NoteBlock type="connection">
Too few mixture components can underfit structure; too many can overfit noise. The appropriate number depends on the data and model-selection procedure.
</NoteBlock>

## 3. Expectation–Maximization

<div class="section-source"><SlideReference src="slides/machine-learning/module-1/fm/chapter-2.pdf" page={76} /></div>

EM alternates between estimating component memberships and updating parameters.

### E step — responsibilities

For sample $x_j$ and component $i$:

$$
r_{ji}^{(k)}
=
P(z_j=i\mid x_j,\theta^{(k)})
=
\frac{
P_i^{(k)}\mathcal N(x_j\mid m_i^{(k)},\Sigma_i^{(k)})
}{
\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}
P_\ell^{(k)}\mathcal N(x_j\mid m_\ell^{(k)},\Sigma_\ell^{(k)})
}
$$

The responsibilities for one sample sum to 1.

<NoteBlock type="blackboard" title="Blackboard example — soft membership">
A sample may currently have responsibilities such as 0.6, 0.3, and 0.1 for three components. The sample is not physically split; these values express uncertainty about its generating component.
</NoteBlock>

### M step — parameter updates

Define the effective membership count:

$$
N_i=\sum_{j=1}^{N}r_{ji}
$$

Then update:

$$
P_i=\frac{N_i}{N}
$$

$$
m_i=\frac{1}{N_i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}r_{ji}x_j
$$

and the covariance by the corresponding responsibility-weighted second moment around the updated mean.

<NoteBlock type="explanation">
EM replaces unknown hard component assignments with their expected values under the current model, then re-estimates the model as if those fractional memberships were available.
</NoteBlock>

## 4. Missing-feature interpretation

<div class="section-source"><SlideReference src="slides/machine-learning/module-1/fm/chapter-2.pdf" page={77} /></div>

The same logic applies when part of an observation is missing. The unknown feature is treated as latent, and expectations under the current model replace unavailable quantities during parameter estimation.

<NoteBlock type="connection">
Gaussian mixtures and missing-feature estimation are both incomplete-data problems. EM provides a common framework because it alternates between estimating hidden information and updating parameters.
</NoteBlock>
