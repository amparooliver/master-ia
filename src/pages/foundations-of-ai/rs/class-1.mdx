# Class 1 — Introduction to AI

This lecture introduces the main ways AI has been defined and motivates the course's focus on rational agents.

<SlideDeck src="slides/foundations-of-ai/rs/01-AI-intro-smaller_compressed.pdf" title="Introduction to AI slides" />

## Essential summary

- AI can be organized along two axes: **thinking vs. acting** and **human-like vs. rational**.
- The four classical approaches are thinking humanly, acting humanly, thinking rationally, and acting rationally.
- The course emphasizes **rational agents** rather than requiring human imitation.
- Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver is an early example of cognitive modeling using means–ends analysis.
- The Turing test operationalizes intelligent behavior through indistinguishability in conversation.
- Logic supports rational reasoning, but representation and computational complexity limit purely logic-based approaches.
- Symbolic and connectionist systems use fundamentally different representations.

## 1. What is AI?

AI spans problem solving, knowledge representation, reasoning, planning, uncertainty, and learning.

A useful way to classify definitions is:

| | Human standard | Ideal/rational standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Thinking** | Thinking humanly | Thinking rationally |
| **Acting** | Acting humanly | Acting rationally |

## 2. Thinking humanly

The goal is to model how humans actually think.

Newell and Simon's **General Problem Solver (GPS)** is a classic example. It uses **means–ends analysis**:

1. compare the current state with the goal state;
2. identify a difference;
3. choose an operator that can reduce that difference;
4. apply it and repeat.

<NoteBlock type="connection">
Solving a problem well and modeling how a human solves it are different objectives. Cognitive modeling cares about the second.
</NoteBlock>

## 3. Acting humanly — the Turing test

Alan Turing's imitation game asks whether an interrogator can distinguish a machine from a human through interaction.

Capabilities associated with the test include natural-language processing, knowledge representation, automated reasoning, and machine learning.

The **Total Turing Test** additionally requires perception and physical interaction, such as vision and robotics.

## 4. Thinking rationally

The “laws of thought” tradition uses formal logic to derive correct conclusions.

A classical syllogism is:

$$
\text{All men are mortal},\qquad
\text{Socrates is a man}
$$

therefore:

$$
\text{Socrates is mortal}
$$

Two major limitations are translating informal or uncertain knowledge into formal logic and computational feasibility.

## 5. Acting rationally

A rational agent selects actions expected to produce the best outcome, or best expected outcome under uncertainty.

Perfect rationality is often computationally infeasible. This motivates **bounded/limited rationality**: acting appropriately given finite time and resources.

## 6. Symbolic and connectionist AI

Symbolic systems explicitly represent knowledge with rules, logic, ontologies, and declarative models.

Connectionist systems learn distributed representations, especially through neural networks.

<NoteBlock type="connection">
Neuro-symbolic AI tries to combine learned representations with explicit symbolic reasoning.
</NoteBlock>
